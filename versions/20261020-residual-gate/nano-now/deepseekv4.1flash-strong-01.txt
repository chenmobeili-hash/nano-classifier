  在做的过程中才慢慢变清楚的。要不这样：先把你要做的这件事，砍到一个五分钟就能完成的小动作，比如只写出标题、

  可以这样想：平均分只告诉你“总体上大概是多少”，但完全没说大家分得散不散。比如两组人平均身高都是170厘米，A组
  每个人都在168~172之间，B组是一半155、一半185。数字上平均一样，可你看到B组会觉得“这群人高矮差得好明显”，看
  到A组会觉得“大家差不多”。平均数像“称出来的总重量”，而数据的“感觉”还取决于它们离中心有多远、有没有极端值、
  是挤在一起还是两极分化。所以光看平均数，就像只看一家餐馆的人均消费，看不出是大家各点各的，还是一个人点了一
  桌、其他人喝水。

  Here's the thing: "random" doesn't mean "smooth" or "evenly spread out." It means each outcome is
  independent and unpredictable one at a time. But when you flip a coin 100 times, you're not asking "what
  will flip 57 be?" — you're looking at the whole pile of results at once, and your brain is a
  pattern-hunting machine. So if you happen to see five heads in a row, that jumps out and feels impossible.
   It isn't. Five heads in a row has a 1-in-32 chance of showing up somewhere — and in 100 flips there are
  lots of places for a streak to hide. Real randomness is lumpy. It clusters, it streaks, it leaves gaps,
  precisely because nothing is correcting it toward "looking fair." A simulation that felt perfectly
  balanced, alternating H-T-H-T with clockwork regularity, would actually be the suspicious one — that's not
   randomness, that's a rule. The patterns you see are real, they're just not meaningful: they're the
  fingerprint of chance, not a message inside it.
