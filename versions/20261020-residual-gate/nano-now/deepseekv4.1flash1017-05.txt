先别把“开始”当成一件要做得完美的事。你可以把计划缩到小得荒唐的一步：今天只花十分钟，把要做的第一件事写成一个动词开头的句子，比如“打开文档，写三行提纲”，然后立刻做掉它。计划不用完整才允许启动；它可以在做的过程中慢慢长出来。今天的目标不是做完，而是让这件事有个“已经开始”的版本。十分钟后回头看，你会更清楚下一步该写什么；要不要我陪你一起把第一步拆出来？

平均数只告诉你“重心在哪”，不告诉你“数据散得多开、有没有极端值”。比如两组人月支出平均都是 5000 元：一组每人都在 4500 到 5500 之间，另一组多数人花 2000，少数几个人花几万。两组的“平均”一样，但第一组给人“都差不多、很稳定”，第二组给人“差距巨大、有个别人特别突出”的印象。所以看数据时，除了平均数，还要看范围、波动大小（比如标准差）和是否有离群值，甚至可以看分布形状——同样是平均 5000，可能是整齐一片，也可能是两极分化。

Here's the thing that trips people up: "random" doesn't mean "looks scrambled to the eye." It means each outcome was decided without favoring anything. Those are different claims, and only the second one is true of a good simulation.

Imagine flipping a fair coin 80 times. Before you start, the exact sequence HTHHTTHT… has a probability of about 1 in 10^24 — the same tiny probability as 80 heads in a row. So "any specific sequence is wildly unlikely" is true, but it's true of every sequence, including the boring-looking ones. What's likely or unlikely is not the individual string; it's the *category* the string falls into. "About 40 heads" covers a huge number of strings. "All heads" covers exactly one. That's why the middle looks normal and the extremes look suspicious — not because randomness avoids patterns, but because there are vastly more unremarkable arrangements than remarkable ones.

The patterns you notice are mostly your brain doing its job too well. Streaks happen: in 80 flips, a run of five or six heads is not a glitch, it's expected. Clumps happen: some numbers on a simulated die will show up four times in 24 rolls. Clustering is what random data looks like; evenly spaced data is what *humans* produce when they try to fake randomness, because we unconsciously avoid repeats.

So when a simulation shows a pattern, ask two questions. First, was this pattern predicted in advance, or did I notice it afterward? Almost every "weird pattern" is found after the fact, and after the fact there are thousands of patterns you could have noticed — one of them was bound to show up. Second, how surprising is it quantitatively? "Six heads in a row somewhere in 80 flips" is common. "Heads exactly 40 times in a specific pre-declared order" is not.

That's also why real randomness needs statistical tests rather than eyeballing: check whether the *counts* and *gaps* match the distribution you expect, not whether the sequence "looks random." A simulation that never produced a streak would be the suspicious one — that's the signature of a bad generator, or of a human typing.
