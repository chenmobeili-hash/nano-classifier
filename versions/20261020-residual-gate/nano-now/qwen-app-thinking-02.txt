[B08] 因为平均数只告诉你“整体有多高”，不告诉你“大家彼此差多远”。比如两组人平均分都是 70：一组每个人都在 68 到 72 之间，成绩很整齐；另一组一半人考 100、一半人考 40，差距极大。只看平均数，这两组看起来一模一样，实际体验完全不同。所以除了平均数，还要看数据的分散程度（比如极差、标准差），以及有没有极端值把平均数拉偏——一组里九个人月薪一万、一个人月薪十万，平均数会被那个人抬高，不代表大多数人的情况。

[B12] Because randomness isn't the same as uniformity. When you flip a fair coin, every single sequence is equally likely — even HHHHHH and HTHTHT. But there are vastly more sequences that look "messy" than ones that look perfectly neat, so messy-looking outcomes happen far more often. That imbalance is what creates patterns.

Another way: random processes love clusters. If you drop dots on a square at random, some areas will end up crowded and some empty, just by chance. Real randomness has clumps. Our brains are wired to notice those clumps and ask "why is that line there?" — but the line appeared precisely because nothing was arranging the dots.

Also, simulations run on rules. Computer "random" numbers come from algorithms, and rules repeat. Given enough tries, repetitions and cycles are guaranteed to show up (that's basically the pigeonhole principle plus the birthday paradox). And when you simulate thousands of trials, rare things become common: a streak of ten heads is unlikely in ten flips, but flip a thousand times and it's practically expected.

So patterns in simulated randomness don't mean the simulation is broken. They mean you're seeing what genuine randomness actually looks like — lumpy, uneven, and full of coincidences that feel meaningful but aren't.
