因为平均数只概括了数据的“中心”，却丢掉了“分散程度”和形状的信息。比如两组人平均工资都是 8000 元：一组人人拿 7500 到 8500，另一组一个人拿 50000、其余人拿 3000——平均数一样，但第二组里大多数人其实很穷。再看极端值和分布形状：少数特别大的数会把平均数拉高，让平均数偏离大多数人的真实体验。此外，两组的平均数相同，一组可能集中在平均值附近，另一组可能分裂成高低两群（双峰）。所以只看平均数，就像只看一本书的目录页码不看内容，印象自然可能完全不同；还需要看中位数、极差和分布才能了解全貌。

Here's the thing about randomness: it's not smooth — it's clumpy. When you flip a coin 100 times, your intuition expects something like HTHTTHTH... a nice even mix. But real random sequences have no memory, so streaks happen. If you've just gotten five heads in a row, the next flip is still 50/50 — it doesn't "owe" you a tail. So long runs appear naturally, and they look like patterns even though nothing is guiding them.

There's a fun way to see this: people asked to write a "random" sequence of coin flips tend to alternate too much and avoid long streaks, while real random sequences have more runs than anyone expects. That's actually how you can catch someone faking data — their "randomness" is too tidy. In fact, the clumpiness of randomness is so reliable that statisticians use the absence of streaks as evidence *against* randomness.

So when a simulation of randomness produces a pattern — a cluster here, a gap there, something that looks like a trend — that's not the simulation failing. That's exactly what genuine randomness looks like. Patterns are the default output of chance, not proof of a hidden cause. The real skill is learning to ask: "Could this pattern appear by chance alone?" — and often, the answer is yes.
