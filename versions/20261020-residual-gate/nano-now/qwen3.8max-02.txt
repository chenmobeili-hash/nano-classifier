计数方法（可复核）：首末位不同分两类——"0…1"与"1…0"。把字符串整体倒置，"0…1"↔"1…0"一一对应，且"恰 4 个 1""无连续三个 1"都倒置不变，故两类数量相等，只需算一类再乘 2。
类 A：形如 0 m₁m₂m₃m₄m₅m₆ 1，中间 6 位恰含 3 个 1，共 C(6,3)=20 种。设 1 的位置集合 S⊆{1,…,6}，|S|=3；整串含"111"当且仅当 S 包含 {1,2,3}、{2,3,4}、{3,4,5}、{4,5,6} 之一，或包含 {5,6}（与末位的 1 连成 p₅p₆p₇=111）。
不合法的 S 恰为：{1,2,3}、{2,3,4}、{3,4,5}、{4,5,6}、{1,5,6}、{2,5,6}、{3,5,6}，共 7 个。
类 A 合法数 = 20 − 7 = 13，总数 = 13 × 2 = 26。
交叉验证（另一条独立路径）：长度 8、恰 4 个 1 且无 111 的总数按"runs"公式 = r=4 的 5 + r=3 的 30 + r=2 中仅 (2,2) 型 10 = 45；其中首末相同者：首末皆 0 等价于长度 6 内恰 4 个 1 无 111 = 6（110110、110011、011011、110101、101101、101011），首末皆 1 时从 {1,…,6} 选 2 个再排除 {1,2} 与 {5,6} = 15−2 = 13，合计 19；故首末不同 = 45 − 19 = 26。两法一致。

想象两个班的平均分都是 75 分。

甲班：几乎每个人都考了 73～77 分。
乙班：一半人考 95 分以上，另一半人考 50 多分。

平均数一样，但这是两个完全不同的世界。原因是：**平均数只告诉你"重心在哪"，不告诉你"数据散得有多开"**。

打个比方，平均数就像一群人站成一排时的"中心位置"。你可以让所有人挤在中心附近，也可以让两个人分别站到很远的最左和最右——中心位置一模一样，可队伍的样子天差地别。

所以看数据时，光有平均数是不够的，还要问三件事：
1. **散不散**：大多数数字离平均值远不远？（这就是"波动大小"）
2. **形状偏不偏**：是左右对称，还是被少数极端值拽歪了？
3. **有没有极端值**：一个特别大的数（比如团队里有一位亿万富翁）能把"平均收入"抬得很高，但那个数字并不代表任何普通人的处境。

一句话总结：平均数是一个"压缩后的摘要"，压缩必然丢信息。两组数据可以压缩出同一个数字，却对应完全不同的现实。所以严谨的说法从来不是"平均是多少"，而是"平均是多少、波动多大、分布长什么样"。

Here's the thing that trips almost everybody up: randomness doesn't mean "evenly spread out." It means "each event doesn't care what the others did." And "doesn't care" is exactly why clumps show up.

Think about it this way. If you dropped 200 grains of sand onto a table, one grain at a time, each landing wherever, would you get a perfect even dusting? No. You'd get little piles, some bare patches, and a few spots where three or four grains landed basically on top of each other. Nothing cheated. The piles are just what "independent" looks like when you actually do it.

In fact, truly even spacing is the *suspicious* result. If I handed you a list of 100 coin flips and it went H, T, H, T, H, T, all the way down, you'd instantly say "that's fake" — and you'd be right. Real coins are rude. They give you five heads in a row for no reason at all.

Now, why does a *simulation* do this too? Two layers:

1. **The math layer.** A computer's "random" numbers come from a formula (a pseudo-random generator). Good ones behave statistically like real randomness — which means they also produce clumps, streaks, and coincidences. If you simulated 1,000 people flipping coins, some of them would flip eight heads in a row. That person isn't special; with 1,000 tries, somebody was going to be.

2. **The brain layer.** This is the bigger one. Your visual system is a pattern-detection machine that spent a few million years being rewarded for spotting the predator in the grass and penalized for missing it. It is *very* good at finding structure, and it does not check whether the structure means anything. Show it noise, and it will happily hand you faces in the clouds, constellations in the stars, and "hot streaks" in a dice log.

So when a simulation of randomness produces a pattern, two things are usually true at once: the pattern is real (it's genuinely in the data), and the pattern is meaningless (it was going to happen somewhere, just from having enough data).

Here's a useful rule of thumb for telling the difference: **ask what happens if you shift your window.** A real signal keeps showing up when you slice the data differently. A coincidence evaporates the moment you look at it from another angle — because you were really just looking at one lucky patch out of a hundred patches you could have chosen.

That's also why "I predicted it afterwards" proves almost nothing, and "I predicted it beforehand, then it happened" is worth paying attention to. Randomness will always give you a story if you let yourself choose the story after seeing the data.

Pretty cool, honestly: the universe doesn't need to be mysterious to look that way. It just needs to be big enough, and you just need to be a pattern-loving animal standing in front of it.
