Here's the thing: when you generate random numbers on a computer, you're using mathematical formulas that are actually completely deterministic. They're not truly random at all. But here's the wild part—even though the process is purely mathematical and rule-based, the numbers it spits out can look completely random to us, and sometimes they even reveal patterns we don't expect.

Think of it like this: imagine a machine that flips a coin 1000 times. Statistically, you'd expect about 500 heads and 500 tails, right? But you might actually get streaks of 10 heads in a row, or clustering of tails. That's not a mistake—that's genuinely what randomness looks like. Our brains are wired to see patterns everywhere because spotting patterns kept our ancestors alive. So when random data has clusters or runs, we think "hey, that's not random!" But that's actually exactly what randomness produces.

So when we simulate randomness in games or simulations, patterns emerge not because the random generator is broken, but because patterns are mathematically inevitable in large random datasets. It's counterintuitive, but learning to recognize and expect these patterns is actually a key part of understanding how randomness really works.
