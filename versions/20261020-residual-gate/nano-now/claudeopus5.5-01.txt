[A10]
先手必胜，唯一能保证获胜的第一步是取 1 枚。
理由：设剩余石子数为 n，轮到行动者时必败的局面称为“必败点”。逐个推算：n=0 是必败点；n=1 不是（取 1 枚到 0）；n=2 是必败点（只能取 1 枚，到达 1）；n=3、4、5、6 都能走到 0 或 2，所以不是必败点；n=7 只能走到 6、4、3，都不是必败点，所以 7 是必败点。依此类推，必败点就是 n ≡ 0 或 2 (mod 7)。
15 ≡ 1 (mod 7)，所以先手处于必胜局面。三种第一步的结果：
- 取 1 枚，剩 14 ≡ 0 (mod 7)，是必败点，留给对手，先手获胜。
- 取 3 枚，剩 12 ≡ 5 (mod 7)，不是必败点。
- 取 4 枚，剩 11 ≡ 4 (mod 7)，不是必败点。
之后每一轮，先手都把剩余数恢复到 ≡ 0 或 2 (mod 7)，就能保证取走最后一枚。

计数方法：4 个 0 形成 5 个空位，记各空位放入 1 的个数为 g1…g5。要求各空位之和为 4，且每个空位 ≤ 2（这样就不会出现 111）。

- 总计 26 个。

平均数只告诉你“大家加起来再平分，每人大概多少”，但它不告诉你这些数是怎么分布的。

举个例子，两个班考试，平均分都是 70 分。
- A 班每个人都在 68 到 72 分之间，大家水平差不多。
- B 班一半人考 100 分，另一半人考 40 分。

平均数一模一样，但你的感受完全不同：A 班很“整齐”，B 班两极分化严重。如果你是 B 班一个考 40 分的学生，“平均 70 分”对你几乎没有意义。

再比如，一个房间里有 9 个普通上班族和 1 个亿万富翁，“平均财产”会高得吓人，可房间里几乎没人真那么有钱。

所以看数据时，除了平均数，还要问：数据有多分散？有没有极端值？大多数人实际在哪儿？这些才决定了数据给人的真实印象。

Here's the funny thing about randomness: real randomness is *supposed* to have patterns in it. It just doesn't have patterns you can *use*.

Flip a fair coin 100 times and you'll almost certainly get a streak of five or six heads in a row somewhere. That feels suspicious, like the coin is "stuck," but it's actually expected. With 100 flips there are lots of chances for a streak to start, and sooner or later one does. If a sequence of 100 flips had *no* streaks and switched back and forth almost perfectly, *that* would be the suspicious one.

Our brains are pattern-detectors. We evolved to notice the rustle in the grass that might be a predator, so we'd rather see a pattern that isn't there than miss one that is. That's why we see faces in clouds, "hot streaks" in basketball, and meaning in shuffled playlists. Spotify actually made its shuffle *less* random at one point, because people complained that truly random shuffles played the same artist twice in a row too often.

Simulations add another twist. Computers usually can't produce true randomness. They use "pseudo-random" generators: formulas that take a starting number, called a seed, and churn out a sequence that *looks* random. A good generator passes tons of statistical tests. A bad one can leave real fingerprints. Some old generators, for example, made points that, plotted in 3D, all fell on a small number of flat planes. So a simulation can show two kinds of patterns:

1. **Fake patterns:** streaks and clumps that are a normal part of randomness, which our brains overinterpret.
2. **Real patterns:** hidden structure from an imperfect generator, which careful tests can catch.

The big lesson is that randomness isn't the same as "evenly spread out." Random stuff clumps. If you ever want to check whether something is random, don't ask "does it have patterns?" Ask "could I use any of these patterns to predict what comes next?" If the answer is no, you're probably looking at the real thing.
