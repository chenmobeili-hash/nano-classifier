[B12] Because a simulation doesn't produce pure chaos—it produces chaos filtered through rules. When you flip a real coin, each toss is unpredictable, but you've decided in advance there will only be two outcomes, each equally likely. That rule is a constraint, and constraints create shape.

Run a thousand simulated coin flips and you'll likely see streaks: HHHH, TTTTT. Your brain goes, "That doesn't look random." But randomness isn't the absence of pattern; it's the absence of predictability. Patterns like streaks, clusters, and near-equal splits are actually what randomness tends to produce. If you forced the results to look evenly mixed—HTHTHT—you'd be destroying the randomness, because real random sequences don't self-correct.

Two more reasons simulations feel patterned. First, most use pseudorandom algorithms: deterministic math that only looks random. Given the same starting seed, they give the exact same sequence—so the "pattern" is literally baked in. Second, averages stabilize. One roll of a die is wild; the average of ten thousand rolls hugs 3.5 very tightly. That's the law of large numbers, and it turns noise into a smooth curve.

So the pattern isn't a bug. It's what happens when you repeat something unpredictable enough times for the rules underneath to show through.
