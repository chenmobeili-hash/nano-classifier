平均数只告诉我们“把总数平均分”后的一个代表值，却丢掉了数据分散程度和形状的信息。比如两组人身高（单位随意）：A 组是 78、79、80、81、82，B 组是 50、50、80、110、110，两组的平均数都是 80，但 A 组人人接近 80，给人“整齐、稳定”的印象；B 组一半人明显偏矮、一部分人特别高，给人“两极分化、参差”的印象。所以看数据时除了平均数，还要看最大值最小值、波动范围（如极差、标准差）和分布形状，才能判断它究竟“长什么样”。

Here's the trick: "random" doesn't mean "messy in every way you can see." A simulation of randomness is usually built from simple rules — flip a coin, roll a die, take the next number from a formula — and simple rules can leave fingerprints. Flip a fair coin 20 times and you'll quite often see runs like HHH or TTTT; that's not the coin being biased, it's just that there are many more jumbled arrangements than perfectly alternating ones, so clusters show up by chance. With a computer it's even more visible: many "random" generators are deterministic formulas, so if you know the formula and the starting seed, the whole sequence is fixed — run it twice and you get identical "random" numbers. There are also subtler patterns, like numbers from a basic generator falling onto hidden lattice structures, or the famous case of people asked to write random digits producing too few repeats because we mistake "random" for "evenly spread." So a good simulation of randomness is designed to avoid patterns we can detect, and statisticians test for them; but the real lesson is that our brains are pattern-hunting machines, and finding a pattern in a short random sequence is usually a story we tell, not evidence that the randomness is broken.
