Randomness doesn't mean "no patterns" — it means the patterns you see were never planned. Imagine flipping a fair coin 100 times. You'll almost certainly get runs: six or seven heads in a row. People usually take that as evidence of "streaks" or a lucky mood, but in any long random sequence, clusters are actually the norm, not the exception. If the coin never produced runs, that would be far stranger and would hint at something non-random.

There's also our own pattern-hungry brain at work. Humans evolved to spot faces in shadows and shapes in clouds, so when a simulation spits out dots that drift, clump, or form a wave-ish line, we immediately say "look, a trend!" The simulation didn't intend the trend; our eyes drew it.

A third ingredient is limited data. In small samples, random chance can lean heavily one way — maybe the simulated dice roll mostly 4s for a while. Probability guarantees that over a huge number of trials things even out (the law of large numbers), but in the short run, streaks and imbalances are not just possible, they're expected.

So the honest answer is: a simulation of randomness produces patterns because (1) uniform randomness inherently clusters, (2) we're wired to find meaning in noise, and (3) short runs haven't had time to smooth out. The pattern isn't hidden order smuggled in by the machine — it's what genuine randomness looks like when we finally get to see it laid out plainly. Once you internalize that, you stop asking "why did it cluster?" and start asking the better question: "is this cluster actually more than chance would produce?"
