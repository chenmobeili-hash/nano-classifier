  Here's something that might surprise you: computers can't actually generate true randomness. When you ask a program
  to "roll a dice" or "shuffle a playlist," it's running a mathematical formula — a deterministic recipe that takes a
  starting number (called a seed) and churns out a long sequence of outputs that look unpredictable.
  The catch? Math follows rules. And rules, even very complicated ones, eventually leave fingerprints.

  Think of it like this: imagine you're trying to fake a random walk by deciding each step yourself. After a while,
  your brain unconsciously settles into habits — maybe you turn left slightly more often, or avoid repeating the same
  move twice in a row. Those micro-habits are your "pattern leaking through."

  Algorithms do the same thing. A common type called an LCG (linear congruential generator) multiplies and adds
  numbers in a loop. It produces billions of outputs before repeating, so it feels endless. But if you plot its
  outputs in 3D, they line up on perfectly parallel planes — a jaw-dropping geometric structure hiding inside what
  looked like chaos.

  Real-world consequences: early versions of online poker software used weak random generators. Researchers figured
  out the pattern, predicted which cards were coming, and won consistently. The randomness looked fine on the surface
  but had hidden structure underneath.

  Even the "random" shuffle on your music app is carefully engineered — pure mathematical randomness would sometimes
  play the same artist five times in a row, which users complained felt non-random. So engineers added rules to
  prevent that, making it less mathematically random but more perceptually random.

  That's the deeper twist: our brains have strong opinions about what randomness should look like, and those opinions
  are often wrong. We expect random things to be evenly spread, never clumping. But true randomness clumps all the
  time — just like how if you flip a fair coin 100 times, you'll almost certainly see a run of 6 or 7 heads in a row
  somewhere. The pattern isn't evidence of bias; it's actually what genuine randomness produces.

  So when a simulation shows patterns, it might mean the algorithm has a flaw — or it might just mean you're seeing
  randomness being authentically, messily itself.
