**先手必胜，且唯一能保证获胜的第一步是取 1 枚。**
理由：记 P 位（轮到谁走谁输）与 N 位。可取 {1,3,4}，递推得 P 位恰为 n ≡ 0 或 2 (mod 7)：0,2,7,9,14,16,…（如 n=2 只能取 1 到 n=1 的 N 位；n=7 的三种走法到 6、4、3 全是 N 位；n=14 到 13、11、10 也全是 N 位）。
15 ≡ 1 (mod 7)，是 N 位，先手必胜。三种首步：取 1 → 14 ≡ 0，是 P 位（胜）；取 3 → 12 ≡ 5，N 位（送给对方必胜）；取 4 → 11 ≡ 4，N 位（同样送胜）。故只有“取 1 枚”正确。
后续维持策略：对方取 1 则我取 4（14→13→9），对方取 3 则我取 4（11→7），对方取 4 则我取 1（10→9），始终把石子数留在 {0,2} mod 7，直到取走最后一枚。

**3882 条**。
容斥法（单调路径数 = 组合数乘积）：
- 总数 C(16,8) = 12870。
- 过 A(2,2)：C(4,2)·C(12,6) = 6·924 = 5544
- 过 B(4,5)：C(9,4)·C(7,4) = 126·35 = 4410
- 过 C(6,3)：C(9,6)·C(7,2) = 84·21 = 1764
- 过 A、B：C(4,2)·C(5,2)·C(7,4) = 6·10·35 = 2100
- 过 A、C：C(4,2)·C(5,4)·C(7,2) = 6·5·21 = 630
- 过 B、C：0（B(4,5) 与 C(6,3) 不可比，单调路径无法同时经过；三重交亦为 0）
结果 = 12870 − (5544+4410+1764) + (2100+630) − 0 = 12870 − 11718 + 2730 = **3882**。
可复核代码（DP，逐格累加，禁点置 0）：
```python
B = {(2, 2), (4, 5), (6, 3)}
f = {(0, 0): 1}
for y in range(9):
    for x in range(9):
        if (x, y) == (0, 0) or (x, y) in B:
            continue
        f[x, y] = f.get((x - 1, y), 0) + f.get((x, y - 1), 0)
print(f[8, 8])   # 3882
```

[B08]
关键在于：平均数只是把一堆数“压扁”成一个代表值，它没说这些数是怎么散开的。

举个具体例子。两个班各 5 人，平均分都是 70：
- 甲班：66, 68, 70, 72, 74 —— 大家都很接近 70。
- 乙班：20, 40, 70, 100, 120 —— 平均也是 70，但有人几乎不及格，有人满分爆表。

如果你只听到“平均 70”，两班听起来一模一样；可甲班是“整齐稳定”，乙班是“两极分化”，给你的印象和该做的决定完全不同。

同样的道理也出现在生活里：某公司“人均年薪 50 万”，可能是一个老板 400 万加四个 10 万的员工，平均数被极端值拉高了；某条路“平均车流 30 辆/分钟”，可能是大部分时间空荡、高峰时堵死。

所以看数据时，除了平均数，还要问三件事：数值散不散（波动范围）、有没有极端值、整体是什么形状。平均数是一个总结，不是全貌——它把差异抹平了，而差异往往才是最有信息量的部分。

Here's the thing that trips almost everyone up: "random" does not mean "evenly spaced out." It means each event is decided without looking at the others. And that independence is exactly what creates clumps.

Picture flipping a coin 80 times. Your gut says it should look like HTHTHTHT — nicely alternating. But real randomness has no memory, so after three heads in a row, the coin is still perfectly happy to give you a fourth. Runs happen *because* nothing is preventing them. In fact, in 80 fair flips, seeing a run of five or six heads is not a glitch — it's expected. If your simulated flips came out perfectly alternating, *that* would be the suspicious result.

Same story with dots scattered on a page. If you drop 200 points at truly random positions, you'll get empty regions and tight clusters. To get an even-looking spread, you'd have to actively push points apart — which means adding a rule, which means it's no longer random. So a good randomness simulator produces lumps, gaps, streaks, and near-duplicates. It's reproducing reality faithfully.

Then there's the other half of the effect: you. Human brains are pattern-detection machines, tuned so hard that they find faces in toast and constellations in scattered stars. Given a wall of noise, your eyes will lock onto the one accidental shape — the streak, the cluster, the coincidence — and it will feel meaningful, because your brain can't help treating it that way. Statisticians even have a name for the general habit of seeing structure in noise: apophenia.

Two more details worth knowing:

- **The law of large numbers is about totals, not sequences.** It says that over a huge number of flips, the *fraction* of heads settles near 50%. It says nothing about the local order. You can have long weird stretches and still land right on 50% overall — the weirdness gets diluted by volume, not prevented.
- **Coincidences are cheap when there are many chances.** One specific unlikely thing is rare. But a simulation generates millions of positions where *something* unlikely could occur, so "at least one surprising pattern shows up" becomes nearly certain. Rare per spot, expected per run.

So when a simulation of randomness hands you a beautiful accidental pattern, it isn't broken and it isn't secretly rigged. It's doing the two things randomness always does: producing clumps because nothing forbids them, and meeting a brain that is wired to notice clumps. The interesting question isn't "is this pattern real?" — it's "how many chances did this pattern have to appear?"
