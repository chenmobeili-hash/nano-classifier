[A10]
先手必胜，且唯一能保证获胜的第一步是取 1 枚（留给对方 14 枚）。
理由：记剩余 n 枚时轮到走的一方，n=0 为必败位（P 位）。递推：若某一步能走到 P 位则该位为必胜位（N 位）。逐个计算得 P 位为 0, 2, 7, 9, 14：
- 2：只能取 1 到 1（N），必败；
- 7：取 1/3/4 分别到 6、4、3，均 N（6→可取 4 到 P 位 2；4、3 可直接取完），必败；
- 9：到 8、6、5，均 N（8→1 到 7；6→4 到 2；5→3 到 2），必败；
- 14：到 13、11、10，均 N（13→4 到 9；11→4 到 7；10→1 到 9），必败。
15 枚时：取 1 到 14（P 位）必胜；取 3 到 12（12 可取 3 到 P 位 9）、取 4 到 11（11 可取 4 到 P 位 7）都把必胜位留给对方。因此只有“取 1”一种第一步必胜，之后始终把 14、9、7 或 2 这类 P 位留给对方即可。

- 经过 (2,2)：C(4,2)×C(12,6)=6×924=5544
- 经过 (4,5)：C(9,4)×C(7,3)=126×35=4410
- 经过 (6,3)：C(9,6)×C(7,2)=84×21=1764
同时经过两个禁止点（按坐标只能自左下到右上的顺序连乘）：
- (2,2) 与 (4,5)：6×C(5,2)×35=6×10×35=2100
- (2,2) 与 (6,3)：6×C(5,4)×21=6×5×21=630
- (4,5) 与 (6,3)：从 (4,5) 到 (6,3) 需向下走，不可能，为 0

因为平均数只概括“中心位置”，却不告诉你数据是怎么散布在它周围的。想象两个班平均分都是 75 分：甲班人人都考 73 到 77 分，乙班一半人接近满分、一半人不及格。平均一样，但一个是“大家差不多”，一个是“两极分化”，给人的印象当然完全不同。决定这种印象的是数据的波动程度（比如极差、标准差），还有些时候是数据的形状——大多数人集中在哪、有没有少数极端值把平均拉高拉低。所以只看平均数，就像只看一个人的身高却不看他体型，会漏掉很多关键信息。

Here's the funny thing about randomness: we expect it to look messy, but it keeps showing us structure anyway. If you flip a coin twenty times, you'll almost certainly hit a run of three or four heads in a row — not because anything is rigged, but because streaks are a natural consequence of not caring about what came before. Each flip forgets the last one, so gaps in the pattern are always chances for a streak to start, and enough chances pretty much guarantee one.

There's another, subtler reason: randomness concentrates. With a fair coin each flip is 50/50, but over 100 flips the *total* number of heads is very likely to land near 50 and very unlikely to be 20 or 80. That's the heart of statistics — individual events are unpredictable, but swarms of them settle into a shape (in the limit, the famous bell curve). So a simulation of random noise, aggregated, almost can't help producing a predictable silhouette.

There's also us in the picture. Our brains are superb pattern-detectors — that's useful for spotting a predator in the grass, but it means we see shapes in static, faces in clouds, and "streaks" in coin flips even when the sequence is perfectly fair. The simulation is just sampling; the drama of the pattern is partly added by the observer.

And finally, real simulations aren't perfectly random. They use deterministic formulas called pseudo-random generators, which cycle through enormous but finite sequences — so if you looked deep enough, patterns genuinely are in there. A good random simulation is a bit like surf: every individual wave is chaotic, yet standing back you see a steady rhythm. Randomness at the small scale and structure at the large scale aren't contradictory — the structure is literally what randomness looks like when it piles up.
