平均数只告诉你"总量摊平后每人分到多少"，它把差别的形状抹掉了。比如两组数据平均都是75分：一组是74、75、76、75——大家挤在中间，印象是"整体踏实"；另一组是100、100、50、50——平均也是75，但你看到的是一半人满分、一半人不及格。平均数一样，故事完全相反。所以看数据除了看平均，还要看散得开不开（方差/标准差）、有没有极端值把平均拽偏（比如99、99、99、3 平均也是75）。平均数是"重心"，不是"全貌"。

Here's the thing: randomness isn't the same as "no pattern." It means every flip or roll is independent — the coin doesn't remember the last ten results. But when you line up a lot of independent results, clumps and streaks are not a bug; they're exactly what randomness looks like.

Imagine flipping a fair coin 8 times. There are 256 equally likely sequences, and "HHHHHHHH" is one of them — just as likely as "HTHHTTHT." So a run of 8 heads isn't the coin "being weird"; it's one specific outcome among 256, and it'll show up about once every 256 tries of 8 flips.

People get fooled because our brains are pattern-hunting machines. We expect randomness to look nicely mixed, like HTHTHT. But truly random sequences are lumpier than that. In 80 flips, a streak of 5 or 6 in a row is actually pretty likely, not a miracle. And if a random generator smoothed everything out — no streaks, perfectly alternating, each digit appearing exactly the same number of times — that would be the suspicious sign, because real independence doesn't balance things on purpose.

So the rule of thumb: the patternlessness is in the process (each event independent, fixed probabilities), not in the appearance. When you see a clump, ask "how many chances were there for a clump like this?" — usually a lot. That's why simulations of randomness produce patterns: patterns are what randomness looks like when you finally get enough of it to stare at.
