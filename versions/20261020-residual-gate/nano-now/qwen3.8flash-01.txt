其实"做得完美"和"开始做"是可以分开的——你现在的卡点不是能力不够，而是想一次性把未知的坑全部填平，这没人做得到。
试试这样：先给自己定一个"丑版本"的目标，比如今天只写 20 分钟，产物允许粗糙、允许推翻。把它写下来，具体到动作（打开文档、写第一段、列出三个待确认的问题），而不是"把计划完善好"。
然后加一条规则：每完成这一小步，就把当时冒出的顾虑记到"以后再说"清单里，不去解决它。你会发现大部分顾虑等你真做起来，要么自动消失了，要么变成一条明确的信息缺口。
最后给个期限锚点：明天这个时间，把 20 分钟的成果发给一个人看（我也可以）。被人看一眼的压力，比自我要求管用得多。
你已经想了很多，说明方向判断其实不差。剩下的部分只能靠"做"来补，先动 20 分钟就够了。

[B08]
因为"平均"只告诉你重心在哪，没告诉你这堆数散得有多开。
举个生活里的例子：两条通勤路线，平均都花 30 分钟。一条每天都 28～32 分钟，稳稳的；另一条有时 10 分钟、有时 50 分钟，平均也刚好 30。前者的 30 是"可靠"，后者的 30 是"你根本猜不到今天会怎样"。感受当然完全不同。
再比如收入：一个班上五个人挣 100、100、100、100、200，另一个班 900、1、1、1、0（总和相同），平均数一样，但" Typical 的人过得怎么样"天差地别——一个数被极端值拽走了，就代表不了大多数人。
还有分布形状：考试成绩可能一半人极低、一半人极高，中间没人，平均"及格"听起来很平静，实际上是个从没出现过的分数。
另外还有"平均值是不是可达的"、"你关心的是最坏情况还是平均水平"——像买伞、看病、存钱，人体验的是最差或最常见的那个值，不是平均。
所以平均数相同，只说明总数一样；分散程度、极端值、形状、频率这些没说的部分，才构成了我们真正感受到的差别。

Here's the thing nobody tells you about randomness: it isn't "evenly spread out." It's just *unbiased*. Those are different.
A fair coin has no memory. It doesn't know you got five heads in a row, and it doesn't owe you a tail. But your eyes are pattern-hunting machines, so those five heads feel like "a streak of heads," something special. Statistically it's just one particular sequence out of many equally likely ones — HHHHH and HHTHT have the exact same probability. You only call one of them a pattern.
Now add more flips. With 10 flips, you'd expect about 5 heads, and rarely exactly 5. With 10,000 flips, you'd expect around 5,000 heads, and here's the cool part: the *absolute* wiggle around 5,000 grows (you might land at 4,950 or 5,060 — that's normal), but the *percentage* gets tighter and tighter to 50%. So long-run average stabilises while local clumps keep happening. Both are true at once.
A simulation makes this vivid because it generates a lot of sequences fast, and you get to watch the "impossible" runs show up exactly when probability says they must. Run enough trials and something wild is basically guaranteed — that's why a lottery coincidence feels shocking but is actually predictable in aggregate.
The deepest version: randomness at scale has structure. Not "no pattern," but "very specific patterns about how often clusters occur." Poisson showed me this by noticing that the number of bombs hitting districts of London in WWII matched a random model almost perfectly. Even chaos had a shape.
So if your simulated dice give you six sixes in a row, nothing broke. You just got closer to the tail of a distribution than you expected. Try it: simulate 10,000 rolls of two dice, graph the sums, and watch the neat bell appear out of pure chaos. That's the moment randomness stops feeling like noise and starts feeling like a law.
