  Here's the thing about randomness: it doesn't mean "evenly spread out." It means unpredictable — and those two ideas
  are surprisingly different.

  Imagine flipping a fair coin a thousand times. You'd expect roughly 500 heads and 500 tails, right? But you'd also
  fully expect some stretches of five or six heads in a row. That's not a glitch — that's randomness doing its job. If
  you never saw any streaks, that would actually be suspicious, like someone was carefully "balancing" the results,
  which would make them less random, not more.

  The human brain is wired to find patterns. It's one of our greatest evolutionary tricks — we spotted the tiger
  hiding in the grass before it spotted us. But that same instinct makes us see shapes in clouds and hear melodies in
  static. When a random sequence produces a cluster of similar results (say, a stock price that rises six days
  straight, or a song that plays twice in a row on shuffle), we instinctively feel like something caused it. Usually,
  nothing did.

  There's also a mathematical reality here: in any long random sequence, you're guaranteed to find patterns by pure
  chance. Given enough coin flips, you'll see ten heads in a row eventually. That's not meaningful — it's just what
  probability looks like when it has room to breathe.

  So when a simulation "looks patterned," it's often working exactly as intended. True randomness is lumpy, streaky,
  and full of coincidences. The absence of patterns would be the real red flag.
