  想象两个班级期末考试的平均分都是75分。

  A班：同学们的分数密密麻麻地挤在70到80之间，大家都差不多。
  B班：一半人考了90多分，另一半人考了50多分，两极分化很严重。

  两个班的平均分一样，但一眼看去，感觉完全不同——A班均衡，B班两极。
  这就是平均数藏住了"散布程度"。平均数只告诉你数据的"重心"在哪，却不告诉你数据有多"散"。两组数据可以共享同一个重心，但
  一组紧紧围绕，另一组天各一方。

  统计学家用"方差"或"标准差"来描述这种散布，这就是为什么看数据时，光看平均数是不够的。

  Here's something that might seem like a paradox at first: you ask a computer to simulate randomness, and it spits
  back something that looks... patterned. How does that happen?

  The short answer is that randomness doesn't mean "evenly spread out." It means unpredictable. And unpredictability
  and pattern are not opposites.

  Think about flipping a fair coin 100 times. Pure chance doesn't guarantee you'll get exactly 50 heads and 50 tails,
  or that heads and tails will politely alternate. In fact, probability guarantees you'll occasionally see runs — five
  heads in a row, three tails, another four heads. These streaks feel like patterns, but they're exactly what
  randomness produces. A sequence with no streaks, perfectly alternating, would actually be the suspicious one.

  Now, computers don't generate true randomness — they use pseudorandom number generators (PRNGs), which are really
  just very complicated math formulas that produce numbers so scrambled they look random. But because they're
  formulas, they're technically deterministic: given the same starting number (called a "seed"), they'll produce the
  exact same sequence every time. That's a structure hiding underneath.

  And here's the deeper thing: our brains are pattern-recognition machines. We evolved to spot a tiger in the grass,
  not to evaluate statistical distributions. So when we see random data, we find patterns — clusters, repetitions,
  gaps — and feel certain something meaningful is there, even when it's just noise doing what noise does.

  So patterns in simulated randomness aren't a bug or a flaw. They're exactly what you'd expect — from both the math
  and the way we see the world.
