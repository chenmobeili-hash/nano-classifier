平均数只告诉你“数据整体重心在哪”，不告诉你“数据散成什么样”。比如两个班平均分都是 75：甲班大多在 70~80 分之间，乙班一半人 50 分、一半人 100 分。平均分一模一样，但甲班让人觉得整齐、稳定，乙班让人觉得两极分化。所以除了平均数，还得看极差、中位数、标准差这些指标，它们描述的是数据“抱团还是撕裂”，以及极端值有多远。

Here's the trick: randomness is a property of *how* a sequence is produced, not of what it looks like. Flip a fair coin 80 times and you'll almost certainly see streaks — HHHHH, TTTT — and lopsided stretches where heads dominate the first half and tails the second. The coin isn't "remembering" anything. It's just that there are vastly more messy sequences than tidy alternating ones, so messy is what you usually get.

Your brain is the other half of the story. It's a pattern-detection machine tuned to spot faces and signals, so it happily reads a short streak as a "trend" and a gap as "due". That's the gambler's fallacy: after five heads, tails isn't overdue — the next flip is still fifty-fifty.

There's a third layer in simulations. Computers usually use pseudo-random generators: deterministic formulas that produce sequences statistically indistinguishable from random ones. Good ones are fine, but a weak generator can leak hidden structure — patterns you'd never expect from "true" randomness.

So a simulation can look patterned because short random runs genuinely cluster, because we over-read those clusters, and because the generator itself may have quirks. Real randomness is lumpier and streakier than intuition expects — that's exactly what makes it random.
