平均数只告诉你这组数的"中间大概在哪"，不告诉你这些数是挤在一起还是散得很开。比如两个班的平均分都是 70 分：一个班人人都考 68 到 72 分，另一个班一半人考 100 分、一半人考 40 分。平均数一样，可前一个班大家水平差不多，后一个班两极分化很严重，给人的感觉完全不同。所以看数据时，除了平均数，还要看数据有多分散、有没有特别极端的值。

Here's the funny thing about randomness: it's lumpier than you'd expect. If you flip a fair coin 100 times, you'll almost certainly get a streak of five or six heads in a row somewhere. That isn't the coin "deciding" anything. With that many flips, streaks are just bound to show up. Our brains are pattern-detectors, built to spot tigers in the grass, so we see those streaks and think something meaningful is going on.

Simulations add a second twist. Computers can't actually be random. They use a formula that takes a starting number (a "seed") and churns out a sequence that looks random. If the formula is good, you'd never notice. If it's weak, it can repeat itself after a while or leave subtle structure behind, like points that line up in neat planes when you plot them in 3D.

So patterns in simulated randomness come from two places: real randomness naturally produces clumps and streaks, and imperfect generators can sneak in patterns of their own. The trick is learning to tell which one you're looking at, and that's basically what statistics is for.
