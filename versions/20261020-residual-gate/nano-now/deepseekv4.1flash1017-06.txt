[B08]
平均数只告诉你“整体重心在哪”，不告诉你“数据散得开不开”。比如两组同学平均分都是 75：第一组几乎人人都在 70 到 80 之间，人人都差不多；第二组一半考 60、一半考 90，平均也是 75，但内部差距大得多。所以看数据不能只看平均数，还要看波动幅度（最大最小、方差这类“分散程度”的指标），否则同样的平均数会给人完全不同的感受。

Here's a way to think about it. When you flip a coin 10 times, you expect about five heads — but "about" is doing a lot of work. The coin doesn't remember being tails last time; every flip is fresh, so any specific sequence like HTHHTHTTHT is just as likely as HHHHHHHHHH. That's the key: randomness doesn't mean "evenly mixed", it means "no memory and no preference". Now imagine running a simulation of thousands of flips. Each run is random, but the *scores* (say, longest run of heads) have a shape: streaks of 7 or 8 heads are unlikely in any single run, but if you run the simulation many times, some run will produce one, and the histogram of streak lengths will form a smooth curve. That curve isn't a conspiracy — it's just what "random with no memory" actually looks like when you count it many times. Same with fake-looking digits: a truly random 120-digit string will often contain "999" or a tidy-looking "123456", because every arrangement is equally likely. Patterns that make you suspicious — long runs, repeated blocks, clusters — are exactly what independent randomness statistically produces sometimes. The trap is that your brain spots the pattern and says "that can't be random", while ignoring the thousands of unremarkable digits around it. That's why randomness tests check *overall* counts and distributions, not whether one string looks pretty. So a simulation producing patterns is normal: randomness guarantees no preference, not no structure — the structure is what you get from repeating a memoryless process many times.
