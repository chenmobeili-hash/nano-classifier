平均数只告诉我们一组数据的“中间位置”，并不描述数据彼此之间差别有多大。比如两个班级的数学平均分都是 70 分。第一班的同学大多在 65 到 75 分之间，成绩比较集中；第二班却有人考了 100 分，也有人只考了 40 分，两极分化很明显。两组数据的平均数相同，但第二班的情况显然更分散、更不稳定。所以，要理解一组数据，除了看平均数，还需要看它的波动范围，比如最高值、最低值，或者数据的分散程度。

Imagine flipping a coin 80 times and getting a run of six heads in a row. It can feel like the coin must be "due" for tails, or that something must be wrong. But a fair coin has no memory, so each flip is independent. Random sequences don't spread out evenly the way our intuition expects. Instead, they clump. Streaks and gaps appear naturally, because a truly random process has no rule preventing them.

This is why simulations of randomness can produce patterns that look meaningful. If you run a simulation many times, you'll see some results cluster and others look surprisingly lopsided. Those patterns are not a sign of a bug. They're exactly what randomness looks like over a finite number of trials. Many people expect "fair" randomness to look perfectly balanced in the short run, but that's a mistake. Balance only emerges slowly, as the number of trials grows very large.

There's also a twist: computers don't generate true randomness. They use formulas that produce sequences that look random but are fully determined by a starting value called a seed. Good generators pass many statistical tests, but the patterns you see in a simulation still come from the mathematics of chance, not from anything intentional.
