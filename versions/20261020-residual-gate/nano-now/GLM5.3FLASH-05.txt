  平均数只告诉你“整体中心”在哪，却不告诉你数据是怎么分布在中心周围的。比如两个小区的平均收入都是每月一万：一
  个是大多数人都拿九千到一万一，另一个是少数人拿十万、大多数人只拿三千——数字一样，生活感受天差地别。这背后是
  “离散程度”在起作用：数据越分散，极端值对平均数的拉动越大，普通个体的体验就越偏离平均值。另外数据的形状也有
  影响：偶尔的极端情况（比如通勤时间平时20分钟、暴雨天两小时）会让人觉得“不可预测”，即使平均值和别处相同。所
  以人类印象更多来自波动、极端案例和分布形状，而不是那个单一的平均数。

  Here's the funny thing about randomness: it's never as smooth as we imagine. Picture flipping a fair coin.
   Most people expect heads and tails to alternate politely—H, T, H, T. But real randomness is clumpy. In
  just 100 flips, you'll almost certainly see a streak of 5 or 6 heads in a row, and that's not a
  glitch—it's exactly what randomness looks like. A simulation repeats these random draws over and over, and
   any limited slice of the results contains coincidences: clusters, gaps, streaks. Our brains, which
  evolved to spot faces in clouds and predators in bushes, jump on those coincidences and call them
  patterns. Sometimes there's a deeper story too: over many trials, the average settles down (that's the law
   of large numbers), even while any short run looks wild and lopsided. So a simulation produces patterns
  not because randomness secretly has a plan, but because chance is naturally lumpy, we only ever see a
  small window of it, and we're pattern-finding machines doing our job a little too enthusiastically.
