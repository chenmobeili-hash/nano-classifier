[B12]Think of randomness as a machine that keeps rolling dice forever. It has no memory and no sense of fairness from moment to moment. So when you look at a small chunk of its output, you often see clumps: five heads in a row, or the number 3 showing up four times in eight rolls. That feels wrong to us, but it's exactly what "no memory" means: after a head, the next flip is still 50/50, so streaks are not corrections, just coincidence.

The pattern you notice is usually real in the data but not meaningful in the cause. Humans are great at spotting shapes and terrible at judging how often shapes should appear by chance. Over 80 coin flips, a run of four or five identical results is quite ordinary. What would be suspicious is a sequence with no streaks at all.

The other catch is sample size. Flip 10 times and the split might be 7–3; flip 10,000 times and it will hug 50/50 closely. Small samples look lumpy, and our brains read that lumpiness as a hidden pattern. Long runs smooth out because many independent coin flips average together, not because the coins start "remembering" what they owe you. So when a simulation looks patterned, ask two things: how small is my sample, and would this pattern actually be unlikely? Usually the answer is "small" and "no".
