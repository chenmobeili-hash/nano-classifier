其实「做得完美」和「开始做」本来就不是同一件事——你现在卡住，是因为想一次性把终点的样子全想清楚，但那个样子只有走到路上才看得见。
不如这样：先给自己做一个「丑版本」。写方案就先写 200 字的烂草稿，做东西就先搭一个只有骨架能跑通的 demo，时间定在 30 分钟内，做完就停。这版唯一的用途是让你有东西可以改，而不是从零想象。
然后再问自己两个问题：这一步最坏结果是什么、它能不能被下一次迭代覆盖掉?通常答案是「能」。完美不是开头做出来的，是改出来的——前提是得有第一个版本让你改。
你要是愿意，把你正在拖的那件事说出来，我们一起把它切成一个 30 分钟就能交差的小块。

[B08]
因为平均数只告诉你「重心在哪」，不告诉你「数据摊得有多开」。
举两个班的数学成绩为例：甲班全是 80 分；乙班一半 60 一半 100。两个班平均分都是 80。可你走进甲班，感觉稳、整齐、老师教的效果一致；走进乙班，感觉两极分化，一半人吃力、一半人很强。同样一个「平均 80」，背后是两种完全不同的现实。
再比如两个城市的月均温都是 20℃，一个四季如春（天天 18~22℃），一个夏天 40℃ 冬天 0℃。你要去住的话，只看平均数就惨了。
统计里描述「摊得多开」的量叫离散程度，常见的是方差和标准差。平均数管「一般水平」，离散程度管「波动大小」，两者一起看，一句话才站得住：光说平均数，容易把「都差不多」和「天差地别」说成同一件事。

Here's the thing that trips people up: a good random simulation doesn't look random — it looks *lumpy*.
Roll a die 60 times and you'd almost certainly see a run like 6-6-6, or long stretches with no 5 at all. If someone showed you that sequence and asked "is this rigged?", the honest answer is: this is what fair looks like. A coin doesn't know it landed heads four times in a row; it has no memory and no urge to "even out." The idea that it must is called the gambler's fallacy, and it's the main reason humans misread randomness.
Patterns show up for a simple counting reason: there are far more sequences containing a streak than sequences avoiding them. With 20 coin flips, the chance you never get two heads in a row is small. Any single specific pattern feels unlikely — and it is — but "some pattern somewhere" is basically guaranteed. That's the difference between "this exact thing is rare" and "nothing interesting ever happens."
There's also the perception part. Your brain is a pattern machine built to find causes. Flip a coin 200 times, plot the running total of heads minus tails, and you'll get a curve that drifts dramatically far from zero, wanders back, then drifts out again. It *looks* like a trend with a story behind it. It isn't. Absolute counts can diverge while the proportion still settles toward 1/2 — that's the law of large numbers, and it says nothing about the next flip.
So when a simulation produces clumps, streaks, hot zones, and dead zones, don't reach for a bug report first. Check whether your random number generator is actually uniform and whether the seeds are independent. If those are fine, the patterns aren't a flaw in the simulation — they're the signature of real randomness, which has never once looked like the tidy, evenly-spread thing people imagine.
