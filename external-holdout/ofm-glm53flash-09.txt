A simulation is a machine that follows certain rules over and over, and randomness gets into it through some seed—a number pulled from a clock or some unpredictable source. Here's the interesting part: even something built to be random will show patterns if you look hard enough, and that's not a bug.

Think about flipping a coin ten times. You wouldn't be surprised to see five heads and five tails, but you also wouldn't be shocked to see seven heads. Seven heads in a row _feels_ meaningful—lucky, even—and our brains can't help noticing it. Statisticians have a name for this: they'll tell you that in a long run of coin flips, streaks of three, four, or five same outcomes are completely expected. A pattern that looks like fate is often just probability doing its ordinary work.

The same thing happens when a computer simulates, say, a thousand random walks on a grid. Each individual walker is unpredictable, but zoom out and you'll see walkers clumping in some regions and thinning out in others—like raindrops on a window that almost never fall perfectly evenly. The clumps aren't a flaw in the random number generator; they're a natural consequence of randomness itself.

There's a deeper puzzle here, too. Computers can't really generate random numbers on their own. They use formulas that produce _pseudorandom_ sequences—deterministic streams that *look* random unless you know the starting seed. So a "simulation of randomness" is really a very long, very complicated piece of arithmetic wearing a disguise. Every so often, if you look at the right slice of the output—the low digits, say, or the sequence in a stepping pattern—the disguise slips and you see order underneath.

So why do patterns show up? Three reasons: first, real randomness genuinely clumps and produces streaks—our intuition about what "random" looks like is simply miscalibrated. Second, when we run many trials, the law of large numbers pulls averages toward expected values, which creates structure at the aggregate level even while individual outcomes stay unpredictable. Third, some simulations cheat slightly by design—deterministic pseudo-random generators trade true randomness for repeatability, and artifacts leak into the results if you set things up wrong.

The lesson isn't that randomness is secretly orderly in some mystical way. It's that pattern and randomness aren't opposites. Randomness produces patterns all the time; the trick is learning which patterns are just noise and which ones are telling you something real. That's actually one of the most useful habits of mind you can build—noticing a pattern, and then asking: "would this show up even if things were totally random?"
