因为平均数只概括了数据的“中心”，却没有反映数据的“分散程度”。举例：两组人各5人，第一组收入是 4900、5000、5000、5000、5100，第二组是 0、1000、5000、9000、10000，两组平均都是5000。第一组里大家都差不多，第二组里贫富差距巨大，生活在两组里的感受截然不同。此外，平均数还容易被极端值拉偏：一个亿万富翁走进小酒吧，会让“人均财富”飙升，但谁也不会觉得酒吧里的人变富了。所以同样（或相近）的平均数背后，可能藏着完全不同的分布形状，给人的印象自然天差地别。

Here's the funny thing about randomness: our brains are pattern-hunting machines, and true randomness doesn't look the way we expect it to look. If you flip a fair coin 80 times, almost everyone's "fake random" sequence alternates H and T too neatly — but a real sequence will contain streaks like HHHHH. Those streaks aren't evidence of cheating; they're guaranteed. With enough flips, some run of six heads in a row is more likely to appear somewhere than not. The same goes for lotteries: someone wins, and if the same person wins twice, that's surprising — but across millions of players and thousands of draws, some unlikely coincidence is bound to show up to *someone*. This is the law of truly large numbers at work: with enough opportunities, even wildly improbable events become expected. So when a simulation produces clusters, streaks, or near-misses, that's not the machine failing at randomness — it's what randomness genuinely looks like when you have a big enough sample. The patterns are real; the meaning we attach to them usually isn't.
