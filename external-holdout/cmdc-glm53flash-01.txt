  [B12]
  Randomness is strange — it refuses to stay smoothly scattered. Flip a fair coin ten times and you'll often
   see runs like HHHHT or TTHTH, clusters that look like someone is "streaking" on purpose. But streaks are
  exactly what math predicts: in a million flips, runs of five or six identical outcomes show up plenty,
  purely by chance. The catch is a well-known human quirk called the clustering illusion: our brains evolved
   to see shapes and structure even where none exists, because missing a real pattern (a predator) was
  costlier than imagining a fake one (a cloud shaped like a lion). Add to that the fact that small samples
  are naturally lumpy — with only a few flips, individual runs dominate the picture — and simulations seem
  to "have a personality." So patterns in randomness aren't a flaw in the simulator; they're a spotlight
  effect created by our pattern-hungry minds plus small-sample noise. That's why real statisticians repeat
  simulations thousands of times: patterns in tiny slices almost always wash away as the sample grows.
